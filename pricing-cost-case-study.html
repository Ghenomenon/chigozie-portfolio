Online Sales Pricing & Cost · Public sample data · SQL · Cost modelling · Scenario and sensitivity analysis

Where does an online shop actually make its money?

A pricing and cost review of 240 online orders across six product categories. Revenue tells one story. Contribution after product cost, payment fees and shipping tells another.

This is an independent analysis of a public sample dataset, not a client engagement. The dataset has no cost data, so every cost figure rests on stated benchmark assumptions. The model is built so real costs can replace them, and a sensitivity check shows which findings depend on them.

Try the scenario explorer ↓View the SQL on GitHub ↗

Business question

Which products and price points carry the business, and where would a discount or a change in shipping cost hurt margin most?

Data and approach

The Online Sales Dataset (Popular Marketplace Data) holds 240 orders, one per day from 1 January to 27 August 2024: category, product, units, unit price, revenue, region and payment method. All amounts are in US dollars.

  • Data checks in SQL. Revenue equals units × price on every row. No missing or impossible values. Two product names needed care on load: one contains commas, one an accented character.
  • One cost view. A SQL Server view joins each order to an assumptions table and adds product cost, payment fee, fulfilment cost and contribution. Every query reads from that view, so a changed assumption flows through everything.
  • Pricing queries. Price profile by category with medians, orders against revenue by price band, monthly trend, revenue concentration, and products sold at more than one price.
  • Cost queries. Cost build-up by category, revenue share against contribution share, and the break-even order value for each category.

One limit shapes every conclusion: each category was sold in a single region with a single payment method. Category, region and payment effects cannot be separated in this data.

Findings and decisions

FindingInterpretation and next decision
Revenue fell 40.9% from January–April ($50,653) to May–August ($29,915)The fall came from fewer high-ticket electronics and appliance orders, not lower prices on the same items. Investigate demand and stock for big-ticket lines before cutting prices.
Items under $50 are 39.6% of orders but 8.4% of revenue. Items over $250 are 24.6% of orders and 65.8% of revenue.Order count is a poor guide to value here. Track revenue and contribution per order alongside volume.
Electronics brings 43.4% of revenue but only 27.9% of estimated contribution, at a 16.2% margin. This holds in 99.9% of cost scenarios.Protect electronics price points. A 5% discount on electronics alone cuts its contribution by about 30%.
Books keep a 19.3% margin after costs. A book order needs about $22 to break even. The exact margin is sensitive to cost assumptions.A flat shipping cost takes a large share of small orders. Test a minimum order value, a small-order shipping charge, or bundles. Books and clothing already sell about three to four units per order.
84 of 232 products make up 80% of revenueRevenue is spread wider than a typical 80/20 pattern. Range decisions need contribution by product, which real costs would allow.

Sensitivity check: which findings survive different costs?

The cost assumptions are the weakest part of this study, so I tested how far the conclusions depend on them. Two checks, both run on the same 240 orders.

1. One assumption at a time

Each bar shows overall contribution margin when one assumption moves across its range and everything else stays at the base case. The line marks the base margin of 25.1%.

Electronics product cost matters most. Its swing of 8.6 points is nearly twice the next largest, because electronics is 43% of revenue. It is the first real number to collect: one supplier price list would remove the biggest single source of uncertainty.

2. All assumptions at once

I ran 10,000 scenarios. In each, every category’s product cost moved independently by up to 10 points either way, shipping fell anywhere from $5 to $11 per order, and payment fees moved by up to half a point.

FindingHolds inVerdict
Electronics earns a smaller share of contribution than of revenue99.9% of scenariosRobust. Electronics product cost would have to be 16 points lower than assumed (64% of price, not 80%) to change this.
A 5% electronics discount cuts its contribution sharplyEvery case testedRobust. The cut ranges from 19% (product cost at 70% of price) to 79% (90%), across the full range tested. The higher the true cost, the more a discount hurts.
Electronics has one of the two lowest margins89% of scenariosRobust. It has the lowest margin in 57%.
Books has one of the two lowest margins75% of scenariosLikely. Small orders stay exposed to the flat shipping cost.
Clothing has the highest margin69% of scenariosLikely. Beauty Products overtakes it in most of the rest.
Books margin is below 20%54% of scenariosFragile. Close to a coin toss. Do not act on the exact figure.

Across all 10,000 scenarios, overall margin stays between 19.9% and 30.2% in nine cases out of ten. The recommendations that rely on robust findings stand: protect electronics prices, and test a small-order charge or bundles. The exact Books margin and the overall margin figure should wait for real costs.

Ranges are uniform and independent, which is a simple choice: real costs often move together. The SQL script includes the one-at-a-time check as a scenario query.

Cost assumptions

These are illustrative benchmarks, chosen to be plausible for each category. They are not the seller’s real costs.

CostApplies toAssumption
Product costElectronics / Home Appliances / Sports80% / 70% / 60% of price
Product costBooks / Clothing / Beauty Products60% / 50% / 45% of price
Payment feeCredit card / PayPal / Debit card2.9% + $0.30 / 3.49% + $0.49 / 1.5% + $0.20 per order
FulfilmentEvery order$8.00 flat

Under these assumptions, estimated contribution is $20,221 on $80,568 of revenue, a 25.1% margin before overheads and marketing.

Limitations

  • Costs are assumed. Margins will move with real supplier prices, fee contracts and shipping rates.
  • Category, region and payment method are fully overlapped, so no regional or payment conclusion is possible.
  • One order per day over eight months is a small sample. Five products sold at more than one price: three marked down and two increased. Treat that as a lead, not a pricing trend.
  • The data has no returns, discounts, customer IDs or marketing spend, so customer value and promotion effects are out of scope.
