Who should receive follow-up after discharge?

Using historical diabetes admissions to explore readmission patterns and how a limited follow-up team could prioritise its workload.

The decision

A discharge team has more patients than it can follow up. Which patients should it prioritise, and what does each capacity choice mean for workload and recorded readmissions captured?

I framed this question through a fictional organisation, Meridian Health Network, and a simulated stakeholder-discovery exercise. The patient records are real historical research data. The scenario does not describe a client engagement or implemented clinical service.

What the results support

Prior inpatient use and discharge destination show the largest descriptive differences. They identify patterns to investigate, rather than proving that either factor causes a readmission.

The model’s test AUC is 0.649. At the 20% capacity scenario, it flags 4,200 of 20,997 test encounters and captures 699 of 1,886 recorded readmissions. Recall is 37.1% and precision is 16.6%. Most flagged patients do not have a recorded readmission.

The operational implication: compare workload and capture at several capacities, then assess whether follow-up is useful for the selected patients. A risk score alone does not establish preventability or intervention benefit.

From findings to a service change

The analysis shows who is at risk. The business analysis asks what would have to change in the discharge process to act on it, and in what order.

Meridian is fictional, so the current process is an assumed baseline built from the discovery questions in the full study. In a real engagement I would validate this map in a walkthrough with discharge planners before relying on it. The evidence column uses results from the analysis above.

How the pilot would be judged

  • 30-day readmission among followed-up patients, compared with a matched group not followed up
  • Share of readmissions in the flagged group, tracked against the 37.1% expected at 20% capacity
  • Process measures: flags with a recorded decision (R4), handover compliance (R6) and outcome recording (R9)
  • Fairness: recall gap between groups closing below 5 points (R8)

Assumptions that affect the decision

The historical dataset covers US hospitals from 1999 to 2008. It does not establish performance in a current hospital or NHS setting. Subgroup differences need further investigation and local validation.

The economic scenarios use hypothetical follow-up costs, prevention rates and avoided costs. They estimate possible outcomes, not measured savings. The model is a retrospective portfolio prototype with no clinical validation or approval for patient-care decisions.

What I would test next

  • Validate discrimination, calibration and subgroup performance on current local data.
  • Confirm which flagged patients could benefit from a specific follow-up intervention.
  • Replace the financial assumptions with measured staffing and service costs.
  • Monitor patient outcomes and workload in a governed evaluation.
